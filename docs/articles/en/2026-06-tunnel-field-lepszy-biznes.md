# A Tunnel Has Walls. A Field Has a Horizon.

**Why faithfully following the procedure can be the most expensive procurement decision in your company**

Two buyers, one task: acquiring a fleet of 50 company cars worth €1.2 million. The first runs the full, eight-step formal tender — market analysis, RFI, RFQ, evaluation committee, price negotiation, legal review, board approval, signature. It takes 180 days. The second works within **the same procurement policy** — competitive price validation, a documented supplier-selection rationale, board approval above the threshold — but chooses the method to fit the situation: an accelerated dialogue with pre-qualified suppliers, a should-cost analysis, direct negotiation with the top-ranked supplier. It takes 45 days. After the audit, both are equally compliant. Only one actually served the company's interest.

That difference — in time, in price, in foregone opportunity — goes uncounted in most organizations. And underlying it is the conflation of two concepts that procurement practice routinely treats as one.

## Policy is not procedure

A procurement policy says **what** must be achieved: authorization thresholds, competitive requirements, documentation standards, ethical rules. A procedure is only **one** of the roads leading to those goals. The relationship is hierarchical: policy sets the constraints and objectives, while a procedure describes a single path through those constraints.

The crucial implication is that for any policy there exists a whole family of valid procedures — many sufficient methods, with widely varying cost, duration and outcome. A routine, low-complexity purchase calls for a different path than a strategic, multi-million acquisition built on a supplier relationship. The trouble begins when an organization treats one chosen procedure as if it were identical to the policy — and in doing so eliminates the entire optimization space that the freedom to choose the method provides.

## Why does the most rigid path win?

If flexibility is cheaper, why do buyers cling to the most formalized road? The answer is an asymmetry of accountability. Deviating from a procedure is visible and auditable — the question "why?" follows immediately. Paying 15% above market "in line with the procedure" prompts no question at all, as long as the documentation is in order. Under that logic, following the procedure becomes the dominant strategy regardless of the consequences for value.

The procedure then serves as a shield: "I did it by the book, so I'm safe." This is how compliance theater is born — a ritual in which simply moving through the steps of the workflow displaces any thinking about the best outcome for the organization. Rigidity is also a rational response to political risk: the more formal the procedure, the smaller the surface for accusations of favoritism. Plain organizational habit plays its part too — "everyone in the industry does it this way" can outweigh any cost calculation, even when "this way" does not produce the best results. But that comfort has a price — and the price can be quantified.

## What does rigidity really cost?

The evidence from procurement economics is instructive here — provided it is read in the right direction. Studies of public markets (Szucs, 2024) show that it is **discretion** in supplier selection that raises prices (a structural effect of roughly **6 percent**; reduced-form ~9%) and leads to contractors of about **10% lower productivity** — so a competitive, formalized tender has real governance value: it averts the favoritism premium. Contractual rigidity, in turn, is **associated** with a **7.7–10.5 percentage point** higher probability of later renegotiation, above a 22% baseline (an observational relationship, not a causal one). In the other direction: an approach based on total cost of ownership (TCO), rather than "compliance first," can — per practitioner heuristics — deliver up to **30%** savings; that is an unattributed practical ceiling spread over several years, not a peer-reviewed result or a guaranteed annual rate.

Taken together, the cost of a rigid procedure can run **100–400% higher** than a policy-based approach. Crucially, the largest part of that gap is not the purchase price itself but foregone optimization, the cost of delayed deployment and — most often invisible in any report — the **cost of bypass**. It arises when an experienced practitioner under time pressure exits the procedure and gets the job done by email, phone and Excel, outside the system and outside control.

That last cost is especially insidious, because it does not vanish from the balance sheet — it merely shifts into the risk column. Inconsistent documentation, a weaker position in a supplier dispute, audit exposure running into hundreds of thousands per single contract. The more rigid the procedure, the more often practitioners exit it — and the larger, though invisible in any report, this hidden bill becomes. The paradox is that a tool conceived as a guarantee of safety generates real, undisclosed risk.

## Six weeks instead of six months

That this is not theory is well illustrated by the case of the operator Swiss Casinos. The company needed to implement an ERP system — a project whose sourcing and contracting, in the classic formal RFP mode, typically take about six months. The team applied a Lean Agile Procurement approach: intensive workshops with pre-qualified vendors, rapid prototype evaluation and direct negotiation. The entire process — from business case to signed contract and delivered proof-of-concept — closed in **six weeks** — about **75% faster**.

What matters most, however, is what the story does *not* contain. There is no breach of policy: competitive evaluation, structured scoring and board approval were all preserved. Only the **method** changed. Cutting more than four months translated directly into earlier ROI on a multi-million investment. The same logic explains decisions that look at first glance like "skipping the procedures" — such as negotiating large fleet contracts in aviation directly with the manufacturer rather than through a rigid tender. That is not a lack of discipline, but the choice of a path appropriate to a market situation that the formal mode simply could not have carried.

## A tunnel or a field?

The simplest way to capture the difference is spatial. A procedure is a **tunnel**: one road, one direction, the human acting as an executor of successive steps. Compliance here is a momentary state — you are either in the tunnel or outside it. When reality departs from the assumptions built into the procedure's design (time pressure, missing data, an unpredictable market), the tunnel fails, and the practitioner faces a choice: force reality into the tunnel at high cost, or exit it. That exit is the bypass.

A policy is a **field**: a space bounded by limits — authorization, competition, ethics and documentation. Within those boundaries any path is valid, and the human is a navigator seeking maximum value. The difference is structural: in the tunnel, compliance is checked at individual checkpoints; in the field, it is checked continuously, at every step.

## Why seal a tunnel no one wants to use?

The natural reflex of an organization that sees people bypassing the process is to "seal the tunnel, make it harder to exit." It is a reaction that fails — and fails predictably. The more rigid the tunnel, the stronger the temptation to escape it when the realities do not fit. More gates, signatures and requirements do not eliminate bypasses; they only push them deeper, out of sight and out of audit, where risk grows rather than shrinks.

The inverse relationship is far more promising. The more paths compliant with the policy, the weaker the incentive to bypass. In the limit, when every sensible path falls within the policy, **there is nothing left to bypass** — the bypass loses its purpose, because the practitioner is already moving inside the permitted space.

## What this changes in the world of procurement systems

Not long ago a rigid procedure was the only available way to enforce compliance — someone had to make sure each step was completed in the right order. Today that function has been taken over by systems. Modern ERP, SRM and e-procurement solutions enforce policy **automatically, at every transaction**: they watch authorization thresholds, competitive requirements and documentation completeness, regardless of which path the buyer takes. Compliance enforcement happens in the background, in real time — not at isolated gates in the workflow.

It is precisely this shift that makes the field model operationally viable. Once policy boundaries are guarded by the system continuously, procedural gatekeeping becomes a redundant, costly add-on. The system takes on control, and the buyer regains what they were hired for: seeking the best value within policy, instead of clicking through stages. Procurement moves from an executing function toward a navigating one — and only then does it begin to return the capital invested in it.

## A question for the board

The question worth asking, then, is not "do our people follow the procedure." It is: "is our policy clear enough that people can freely seek the best value within its boundaries — and can our systems enforce that policy?" A tunnel offers an illusion of control. A field delivers a result.

A tunnel has walls. A field has a horizon.
